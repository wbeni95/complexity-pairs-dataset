## 1. Technique taxonomy

The controlled vocabulary (38 techniques plus 9 kinds of slow baseline) is in
`research/2026-10-07_technique_mapping.json`. This section explains the techniques that the dataset uses, grouped
by the structural property they exploit. "Entries" lists the ids whose slow-to-fast transformation uses the
technique ((p) = provisional record for an entry added during the session).

### 1.1 The techniques

**A. Reuse of shared subproblems**

1. **memoization-dp — Memoisation / dynamic programming.** Solve each distinct subproblem once and combine the
   stored answers by a recurrence. *Exploits:* optimal substructure, plus a set of distinct subproblems far
   smaller than the recursion tree or the solution space. *Entries:* fibonacci (DP step), edit-distance, lcs,
   longest-increasing-subsequence (quadratic DP), matrix-chain, maximum-subarray (Kadane = one-state DP),
   all-pairs-shortest-paths (p) (Floyd–Warshall shares intermediate-vertex subproblems across sources),
   knapsack (capacity DP, pseudo-polynomial, recorded as a peer), the 4 synthetic linear recurrences.
2. **subset-dp — DP over subsets.** The state is a subset plus O(1) boundary information. *Exploits:* the value
   of a partial solution depends on which elements are used, not on their order (n! → 2^n·poly). *Entries:*
   tsp (Held–Karp), and the slow side of chromatic-number (p) (3^n subset DP).
3. **incremental-reuse — Reuse the previous result.** *Exploits:* nested or additive quantities over overlapping
   ranges. *Entries:* maximum-subarray (running sums), longest-palindromic-substring (p) (expand around centers),
   permanent (Gray-code update inside Ryser).

**B. Decomposition and recombination**

4. **divide-and-conquer.** *Exploits:* a partition with a cheap merge (sorted halves, strip lemma, cross
   inversions). *Entries:* sorting (merge sort), inversion-counting, closest-pair, element-distinctness (p) (via merge sort).
5. **meet-in-the-middle.** *Exploits:* additivity of the objective across a split of the variables, so that
   combining is a one-dimensional search. *Entries:* knapsack (Horowitz–Sahni), discrete-logarithm (baby-step
   giant-step, staging).
6. **bilinear-recursion — fewer multiplications in a fixed-size bilinear identity, applied recursively.**
   *Exploits:* the tensor rank of a small format is below the naive count, and recursion turns rank r of format
   k into exponent log_k r. *Entries:* integer-multiplication (Karatsuba, rank 3 instead of 4),
   matrix-multiplication (Strassen, rank 7 instead of 8). This is the one technique in the dataset whose
   fixed-size discoveries amplify into asymptotic statements (Section 4).

**C. Algebraic structure**

7. **algebraic-transform — FFT / NTT / Walsh–Hadamard / zeta–Möbius.** *Exploits:* the characters of a group
   (or the subset lattice's zeta/Möbius pair) diagonalise the operation, and the transform factorises into
   O(log N) sparse stages. *Entries:* polynomial-multiplication (NTT); also used inside chromatic-number (p) and
   forrelation (p, classical side). Candidates 1–2 below add the Boolean-cube versions.
8. **elimination-invariant.** *Exploits:* the target (determinant, rank, solution space) is invariant under
   elementary row operations. *Entries:* determinant (Gaussian elimination); simon (GF(2) post-processing).
9. **inclusion-exclusion.** *Exploits:* a covering-type constraint (every column or vertex used), with cheap
   unconstrained counts on subsets. *Entries:* permanent (Ryser), chromatic-number (p) (Björklund–Husfeldt–Koivisto).
10. **algebraic-characterization — a polynomial identity checked in a small ring.** *Entries:* both primality
    entries (AKS).
11. **invariant-reduction — Euclidean descent.** *Entries:* gcd.
12. **repeated-squaring — doubling in a monoid.** *Exploits:* associativity. *Entries:* modular-exponentiation,
    fibonacci (fast doubling), the synthetic linear recurrences (companion matrix), range-minimum-queries (p)
    (the doubling construction of the sparse table); also inside Miller–Rabin, AKS and Shor.

**D. Order, greed and duality**

13. **greedy-exchange.** *Exploits:* matroid or cut structure (MST), and the finality of minimum labels under
    non-negative weights (Dijkstra). *Entries:* minimum-spanning-tree, shortest-path.
14. **sorting-monotone-scan.** *Exploits:* after sorting, the predicate is monotone in the index, or equal items
    are adjacent. *Entries:* three-sum, longest-increasing-subsequence (patience sorting), element-distinctness (p);
    also inside Kruskal and the knapsack meet in the middle.
15. **primal-dual-potentials** and **augmenting-paths.** *Exploits:* LP integrality and duality; optimality
    holds iff there is no improving path. *Entries:* assignment (Hungarian). Augmenting paths also appear on the
    slow side of bipartite-matching (p) (Kuhn) and max-flow (p) (Edmonds–Karp).
16. **phased-augmentation — blocking flows.** *Exploits:* residual s–t distances grow monotonically from phase
    to phase. *Entries:* bipartite-matching (p) (Hopcroft–Karp), max-flow-edmonds-karp-vs-dinic (p).
17. **continuous-optimization** and **preflow-push.** *Entries:* the LP and max-flow staging entries.

**E. Scanning and data structures**

18. **amortized-scan** and **automaton.** *Exploits:* self-similarity of the pattern (borders, palindromic
    mirrors) and regularity (finitely many relevant states). *Entries:* string-matching (KMP: both),
    longest-palindromic-substring (p) (Manacher: amortized-scan). Only one entry uses `automaton`.
19. **data-structure.** *Entries:* range-minimum-queries (p) (sparse table: min is associative and idempotent).
    Union-find appears only inside Kruskal.

**F. Randomisation (classical)**

20. **random-witness.** *Exploits:* witnesses are abundant. *Entries:* primality-miller-rabin-vs-aks,
    polynomial-identity-testing (staging), deutsch-jozsa (p) (classical randomized side).
21. **random-collision.** *Exploits:* a hidden 2-to-1 structure (the birthday bound). *Entries:* the classical
    side of simon and collision (p); Pollard rho in the staging factoring and discrete-log entries.
22. **randomized-search, random-order, random-sampling-estimation.** *Entries:* 3sat-brute-force-vs-schoening (p)
    (Schöning's walk); boolean-satisfiability (Schöning, PPSZ; staging); the classical simulation side of
    forrelation (p).

**G. Quantum and dequantisation**

23. **quantum-fourier-sampling.** *Exploits:* the hidden object is a character or a coset structure of an
    abelian group. *Entries:* bernstein-vazirani, deutsch-jozsa (p), simon, forrelation (p), and Shor in
    integer-factoring and discrete-logarithm (staging).
24. **amplitude-amplification.** *Entries:* grover, collision (p) (Brassard–Høyer–Tapp), minimum-finding (p)
    (Dürr–Høyer).
25. **quantum-walk** (element-distinctness-quantum-walk, p), **quantum-linear-algebra** (Kerenidis–Prakash;
    linear-systems-hhl, p), **length-squared-sampling** (Tang, T5; also the low-rank classical algorithm in
    linear-systems-hhl, p).
26. **group-theoretic** (graph isomorphism) and **sieving-smoothness** (number field sieve): staging only.

**Vocabulary items that no entry uses yet:** `branch-and-reduce`, `monotone-matrix-search` and
`determinant-counting` [exp: dataset_stats]. `structured-linear-algebra` appears only as the classical baseline
of linear-systems-hhl (p). They are in the vocabulary because the candidates in Section 3 need them.

### 1.2 Two kinds of slow baseline, and when memoisation is enough (measured)

The mapping labels 48 algorithms in pairs/ and staging/ with a baseline kind (the slow side; also the
best-known-classical intermediates of the open problems and the quantum-claimed side of the T5 entry)
[exp: dataset_stats]: exhaustive enumeration 20, classical query baseline 8, best known classical algorithm 4,
unmemoised recursion 4, value-linear iteration (polynomial in the value, exponential in the bit length) 4,
direct definition (schoolbook formula) 3, simple polynomial algorithm 3, repeated subroutine 1, quantum-claimed 1.

For the recursive baselines, the decisive structural quantity is the number of distinct subproblems. I counted it
in the unchanged dataset implementations by tracing their recursive calls with `sys.setprofile`
[exp: subproblem_redundancy]:

| Entry (slow implementation) | Memo key | Distinct keys (measured) | Calls at the largest n |
|---|---|---|---|
| fibonacci naive | n | n + 1 (n = 10..22) | 57,313 at n = 22 |
| synthetic linear-recurrence c1-1-1 | n | n + 1 | 433,993 at n = 22 |
| edit-distance plain recursion | (i, j) | (n + 1)² (n = 2..8) | 398,593 at n = 8 (81 keys) |
| matrix-chain plain recursion | (i, j) | n(n + 1)/2 (n = 4..12) | 177,147 = 3^11 at n = 12 (78 keys) |
| determinant cofactor expansion | remaining column set | **2^n exactly** (n = 3..8) | 109,601 at n = 8 (256 keys) |
| shortest-path DFS | (vertex, visited set) | **n·2^(n−3) + 1** (n = 4..9) | 27,400 at n = 9 (577 keys); vertex alone: n keys |

(The closed forms were read off the measured tables, not proven.)

**Pattern.** "Memoise the recurrence" yields a polynomial algorithm only when the distinct keys are polynomially
many: the first four rows. For the determinant, memoisation of the natural key gives about 2^n states. The
polynomial algorithm needs a different property, row-operation invariance. For shortest paths, the polynomial
algorithm needs a *state compression* (drop the visited set), which is valid because non-negative weights make
some shortest walk simple. Both are structural insights beyond memoisation. A learned "un-bloater" that only
memoises would solve the first four rows and fail on the last two. This is a measurable version of the
START_HERE section 5 warning.

### 1.3 Patterns across tags (from the mapping)

Primary tag × transformation technique over pairs/ and staging/ [exp: dataset_stats]:

- **T2 (naive exponential → polynomial):** memoization-dp 5, greedy-exchange 2, repeated-squaring 2, and one
  each of elimination-invariant, invariant-reduction, primal-dual + augmenting-paths, sorting-monotone-scan.
- **T3 (polynomial → faster polynomial):** divide-and-conquer 3; amortized-scan, bilinear-recursion,
  incremental-reuse, memoization-dp, phased-augmentation and sorting-monotone-scan 2 each; algebraic-transform,
  automaton, data-structure, continuous-optimization and preflow-push 1 each.
- **T6 (open problems, most with a T8 secondary):** inclusion-exclusion 2, meet-in-the-middle 2,
  randomized-search 2, subset-dp 1, plus the sub-exponential, quantum, quantum-linear-algebra and group-theoretic
  techniques of the open problems.
- **T9 (quantum query separations):** quantum-fourier-sampling 4, amplitude-amplification 3, quantum-walk 1,
  random-witness 1 (the classical randomized side of Deutsch–Jozsa).

Four of the five T8 pairs in pairs/ (knapsack, permanent, TSP, chromatic number) rely on one of three
properties: order-independence (subset DP), covering structure (inclusion–exclusion) or additive separability
(meet in the middle). The fifth, 3-SAT (Schöning, added during this session), uses randomized local search. The
T8 entries in staging add group theory (graph isomorphism) and collisions and sieving (factoring). This
motivates hypothesis I3 in Section 7.

## 3. Transfer candidates

Selection rules: the problem is not in the dataset; it is not a target of tonight's other agents (all-pairs
shortest paths, longest palindromic substring, classical element distinctness, chromatic number, range-minimum
queries, Kuhn vs Hopcroft–Karp, Edmonds–Karp vs Dinic, 3-SAT brute force vs Schöning, Deutsch–Jozsa, BHT
collision, Dürr–Høyer, Ambainis element distinctness, Forrelation, HHL, the flip-graph search, sorting networks);
and it exploits a structural property that the taxonomy identifies.

Ranking criteria, in order of weight:
1. adds a technique or domain the dataset lacks;
2. V2 can resolve the gap (ρ below);
3. a cheap exact or independent verifier exists;
4. low implementation risk in pure Python;
5. verified source;
6. trap severity.

Six candidates were probed with minimal implementations [exp: feasibility_probes]. Those rows are marked
**probe**. Every other feasibility statement is an estimate, labelled "est."

**ρ** = least-squares slope of log g against log f over the n range on which the fast algorithm would be timed
[exp: v2_resolvability]. It is the α that noise-free data following the SLOW cost g would produce in a fit
against the FAST claim f. **caught** means |ρ − 1| > 0.25: a fast implementation that secretly ran in g would
fail V2. The n ranges assume 1e-7 s per unit of cost (rough). For pure power laws and pure exponentials ρ does
not depend on the range. Sanity rows reproduce known findings: Strassen ρ = 1.069 (not caught, as in RL-006),
Karatsuba 1.262 (caught narrowly, as that entry's caveat says), n log n vs n 1.095 (not caught, as in RL-018).

### 3.2 Details per candidate

Each block gives: costs and their basis; source; verifier; pure-Python feasibility; difficulty; traps.

**1. XOR (Walsh–Hadamard) convolution mod p.** h(k) = Σ_{i⊕j=k} a(i)b(j) for vectors of length N = 2^n.
- *Costs:* naive Θ(4^n) = Θ(N²); fast Walsh–Hadamard transform (FWHT) Θ(n·2^n) = Θ(N log N): three transforms
  and a pointwise product.
- *Source:* Fino & Algazi 1976, *Unified Matrix Treatment of the Fast Walsh-Hadamard Transform*, IEEE Trans.
  Computers, doi:10.1109/TC.1976.1674569 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* the naive convolution is an exact oracle. Cheap independent identities: Σ_k h(k) = (Σa)(Σb) and
  h(0) = Σ_i a(i)b(i).
- *Probe:* 0/24 disagreements (n = 0..7). Naive α = 0.987 against 4^n (n = 5..9); FWHT α = 0.994 against n·2^n
  (n = 9..14), and 0.560 when fitted against 4^n, so the gap is resolved.
- *Why it ranks first:* a second instance of `algebraic-transform` on a different group (Z_2^n instead of Z_p).
  It is also the classical twin of the H^⊗n step of Bernstein–Vazirani, Deutsch–Jozsa and Forrelation (computing
  Forrelation's Φ exactly is one FWHT), so it links classical and quantum entries.
- *Traps:* state costs in N to avoid an "exponential" misreading (the input has N entries, so this is T3, not
  T8); N log N vs N is not resolvable (log factor); the modular inverse of N needs p odd.

**2. Zeta transform, with subset convolution as an extra algorithm.** ζf(S) = Σ_{T⊆S} f(T); subset convolution
h(S) = Σ_{T⊆S} f(T)g(S∖T).
- *Costs:* zeta: naive over submasks Θ(3^n) → Yates Θ(n·2^n). Subset convolution: naive Θ(3^n) → ranked zeta and
  Möbius Θ(n²·2^n).
- *Sources:* Björklund, Husfeldt, Kaski & Koivisto 2007, *Fourier meets Möbius: fast subset convolution*, STOC,
  doi:10.1145/1250790.1250801 [DOI OK]; Yates 1937 [book, unverified].
- *Probe:* 0/27 and 0/33 disagreements. Zeta: naive α = 1.033 against 3^n, Yates 1.012 against n·2^n and 0.710
  against 3^n, so resolved. Subset convolution: ranked α = 0.983 against n²·2^n, but **0.836 against 3^n, inside
  the tolerance**, so the timing cannot separate the two claims at n ≤ 11–12. This matches the analytic
  ρ = 1.205: the n² factor eats most of the gap at small n.
- *Proposal:* make the entry about the zeta transform (V2) and record subset convolution as an extra pair at V1,
  with this caveat. Alternatively, use exact operation counts (Section 7, idea I1).
- *Traps:* state N = 2^n as the input size (T3); arithmetic mod p.

**3. Regular-expression matching: backtracking vs Thompson NFA simulation.** Restricted syntax: a concatenation of
atoms c, c?, c* (no backreferences); full match.
- *Costs:* backtracking (Perl-style, tries "consume" first) on P_n = (a?)^n a^n against a^n. The probe counted
  11, 63, 319, 1535, 7167, 32767, 147455, 655359, 2883583, 12582911 steps for n = 2, 4, …, 20, which equals
  (n/2 + 2)·2^n − 1 at every measured n (read off the data, not proven), i.e. Θ(n·2^n). Thompson's simulation
  costs Θ(m·|text|) = Θ(n²).
- *Source:* Thompson 1968, *Programming Techniques: Regular expression search algorithm*, CACM,
  doi:10.1145/363347.363387 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* Python's `re.fullmatch` on small random patterns. The probe found 0/320 disagreements after fixing
  a bug in my own Thompson closure (recorded in the probe's docstring).
- *Probe timing:* backtracking α = 1.072 against 2^n (the claim should be stated as n·2^n); Thompson α = 0.997
  against n².
- *Why:* it adds `automaton` to a second problem and is real-world relevant (backtracking engines are deployed).
  Memoising backtracking on (atom index, text position) gives a third, Θ(mn) algorithm, which links this
  candidate to memoisation.
- *Traps:* the exponential cost is family-specific, so the family must be fixed with both pattern and text
  growing in n; backreferences change the complexity class [recalled: NP-hard with backreferences], so exclude
  them; Python's `re` must be only the oracle (C speed, implementation-specific).

**4. Global minimum cut.**
- *Costs:* brute force over all 2^(n−1) − 1 bipartitions Θ(2^n·n²) → Stoer–Wagner (array) Θ(n³). Karger–Stein
  (randomized, Monte Carlo) as a third algorithm.
- *Sources:* Stoer & Wagner 1997, *A simple min-cut algorithm*, J. ACM, doi:10.1145/263867.263872 [DOI OK];
  Karger & Stein 1996, *A new approach to the minimum cut problem*, J. ACM, doi:10.1145/234533.234534 [DOI OK].
- *Exploits:* the maximum-adjacency ordering makes the last "cut of the phase" a minimum s–t cut for the last two
  vertices, which are then merged (`greedy-exchange`-like argument; a new problem for the graph domain).
- *Probe:* 0/45 disagreements. Brute force α = 0.951 against 2^n·n²; Stoer–Wagner α = 0.922 against n³.
- *Traps:* Stoer–Wagner (n³) vs Karger–Stein (n² log³ n) gives ρ = 1.050, not resolvable, so Karger–Stein stays at
  V1, and its V1 check is probabilistic. Beyond n ≈ 13 the independent oracle would be n − 1 max-flow
  computations; the new max-flow entry (Edmonds–Karp vs Dinic) could supply them.

**5. NAND-tree evaluation (query model).** A complete binary NAND tree of height h with N = 2^h leaves.
- *Costs:* left-first short-circuit evaluation reads all 2^h leaves on the "right-zero reluctant" inputs
  (probe: exactly 2^h for h = 6..20). Random-child-order evaluation has expected cost growing like λ^h with
  λ = (1+√33)/4 ≈ 1.686, i.e. N^0.7537. I derived this recurrence for reluctant inputs
  (R0(h) = 2R1(h−1), R1(h) = R0(h−1) + R1(h−1)/2), and the probe confirms it: α = 1.011 against λ^h on means of
  40 instances per h.
- *Sources:* Saks & Wigderson 1986, *Probabilistic Boolean decision trees and the complexity of evaluating game
  trees*, FOCS, doi:10.1109/SFCS.1986.44 [DOI OK]. The claim that λ^h is optimal for randomized algorithms is
  [recalled]. Quantum: Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann 2008, *A Quantum Algorithm for the Hamiltonian NAND Tree*,
  Theory of Computing 4, doi:10.4086/toc.2008.v004a008: Crossref has no title for it; year 2008, volume 4,
  pages 169–190 are registered.
- *Verifier:* bottom-up full evaluation (0/110 disagreements).
- *Traps:* (a) **tag decision:** randomisation changes the exponent here (deterministic N vs randomized N^0.754,
  both with proven bounds [recalled]); T4 says "same class, different model" and T9 is quantum-only, so the
  maintainer must choose (my suggestion: T4 primary, T3 secondary, with `lower_bounds` for both models);
  (b) random leaves are far easier than reluctant inputs, so worst-case families must be generated; (c) the probe
  fit of random-order counts against 2^h gives 0.762, inside the tolerance, so only the direction that matters
  (a secretly-2^h implementation would fail, ρ = 1.327) is resolved. The margin is small.

**6. Optimal binary search tree.**
- *Costs:* plain recursion over the root choice; my estimate is Θ(3^n), because the recurrence has the same shape
  as matrix chain (RL-007), but this should be confirmed by call counting. The standard DP is Θ(n³); Knuth's
  monotone-root speed-up gives Θ(n²).
- *Sources:* Knuth 1971, *Optimum binary search trees*, Acta Informatica, doi:10.1007/BF00264289 [DOI OK]; Yao
  1980, *Efficient dynamic programming using quadrangle inequalities*, STOC, doi:10.1145/800141.804691 [DOI OK].
  That Knuth's paper gives the O(n²) algorithm is [recalled] (standard textbook fact).
- *Verifier:* recursion for small n; the Θ(n³) DP is an independent algorithm for checking the Θ(n²) one.
- *Why:* introduces `monotone-matrix-search` (quadrangle inequality), a family absent from the dataset, in a
  three-level chain (T2 then T3).
- *Traps:* the speed-up needs the quadrangle inequality (it holds for non-negative access frequencies); the n²
  bound is amortised by telescoping, so worst-case instance families matter little, but V2 should use random
  frequencies plus skewed ones.

**7. Longest common substring.**
- *Costs:* DP Θ(n²) → suffix automaton of a, then scan b, Θ(n) for a fixed alphabet.
- *Source:* Blumer et al. 1985, *The smallest automaton recognizing the subwords of a text*, TCS,
  doi:10.1016/0304-3975(85)90157-4 [DOI OK] (Crossref spells the title "automation").
- *Verifier:* O(n³) brute force; 0/104 disagreements.
- *Probe:* DP α = 1.009 against n². Suffix automaton **α = 1.239 against n (close to the 1.25 limit)**; dict-per-state
  memory effects are the likely cause, as with the Kruskal tuples in RL-018.
- *Why:* forms a natural boundary with lcs-brute-vs-dp: common *substring* is linear, common *subsequence* is
  quadratic under SETH (that entry's lower bound).
- *Traps:* use flat arrays or a smaller n range for V2; the alphabet size enters the cost (O(n·σ) with arrays).

**8. Multi-pattern matching (Aho–Corasick).**
- *Costs:* naive Θ(n·Σ|P_i|) → Θ(n + Σ|P_i| + occ). With text a^n and k = √n patterns a^(m−1)b with m = √n, the
  naive matcher does Θ(n²) work and Aho–Corasick Θ(n).
- *Source:* Aho & Corasick 1975, *Efficient string matching: an aid to bibliographic search*, CACM,
  doi:10.1145/360825.360855 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* naive; or k runs of the existing KMP implementation (independent code).
- *Why:* the direct transfer of KMP's border structure to a pattern set, and a second `automaton` entry.
- *Traps:* the output size occ can be Θ(nk), so count occurrences instead of listing them; two size parameters,
  so fix k and m as functions of n.

**9. 2-SAT.**
- *Costs:* brute force Θ(2^n m) → implication graph plus strongly connected components Θ(n + m); Papadimitriou's
  random walk O(n²) expected steps on satisfiable formulas [recalled].
- *Sources:* Aspvall, Plass & Tarjan 1979, IPL, doi:10.1016/0020-0190(79)90002-4 [DOI OK] (already in
  notes/boundary-2sat-vs-3sat.md); Papadimitriou 1991, *On selecting a satisfying truth assignment*, FOCS,
  doi:10.1109/SFCS.1991.185365 [DOI title OK, no year] (the proceedings name gives 1991).
- *Verifier:* check a returned assignment against all clauses; for UNSAT, x and ¬x in one SCC is a certificate.
- *Why:* turns the 2-SAT/3-SAT boundary note into a validated pair next to the SAT work tonight; the random walk
  is the 2-SAT ancestor of Schöning's 3-SAT walk.
- *Traps:* brute force is worst case only on UNSAT (or late-SAT) instances, and random 2-SAT with m/n large is
  UNSAT with high probability [recalled]; the random walk's V1 check is probabilistic; the T4 secondary tag
  applies only if the maintainer accepts "randomized O(n²) vs deterministic O(n+m)" under T4.

**10. Counting spanning trees (matrix-tree theorem).**
- *Costs:* enumeration of (n−1)-edge subsets (as in the MST entry) → det of a reduced Laplacian, Θ(n³).
- *Source:* Kirchhoff 1847, Annalen der Physik, doi:10.1002/andp.18471481202 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* Cayley's n^(n−2) for K_n [entry, MST notes]; deletion–contraction (independent, exponential) for
  small graphs.
- *Why:* introduces `determinant-counting` and links to the determinant/permanent contrast already in the
  dataset.
- *Traps:* the count is an integer that can exceed p, so either compute exactly (Bareiss, bit-size growth) or
  define the problem mod p; the brute force needs n ≤ 7 on dense graphs (as in the MST entry), so sparse graphs
  give longer V1 ranges.

**11. LCS of two permutations: Hunt–Szymanski.**
- *Costs:* Θ((r + n) log n) with r matching pairs; r = n for permutations, so Θ(n log n) against the Θ(n²) DP.
- *Source:* Hunt & Szymanski 1977, CACM, doi:10.1145/359581.359603 [DOI OK].
- *Why:* a pair whose separation exists only on a restricted family and is stated in an output parameter
  (the dataset's patience sorting is already O(n log L), but its claim is made for the worst case). It does not
  contradict the SETH bound recorded in lcs-brute-vs-dp, because r = Θ(n²) in the worst case.
- *Traps:* the claim must be restricted to the permutation family (or stated in r); the log factor is not
  resolvable (only n² vs n log n is).

**12. Berlekamp–Massey.**
- *Costs:* minimal-order linear recurrence of a length-n sequence over GF(p): solving Hankel systems for
  increasing orders by elimination (Θ(n³) per order, or Θ(n³) total with incremental elimination; to be fixed in
  the entry) → BM Θ(n²).
- *Source:* Massey 1969, *Shift-register synthesis and BCH decoding*, IEEE Trans. IT,
  doi:10.1109/TIT.1969.1054260 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* regenerate the sequence from the returned recurrence; check minimality by the Hankel rank.
- *Why:* `structured-linear-algebra`; also a verifier tool for generators (it recovers a generated recurrence
  from its output).
- *Traps:* define the slow baseline precisely (a straw-man n⁴ variant would be T7-like).

**13. Fiduccia's algorithm (k-th-order recurrences).**
- *Costs:* a(N) for an order-k recurrence: companion-matrix power Θ(k³ log N) → x^N mod the characteristic
  polynomial Θ(k² log N) with schoolbook polynomial arithmetic.
- *Source:* Fiduccia 1985, *An Efficient Formula for Linear Recurrences*, SIAM J. Comput.,
  doi:10.1137/0214007 [DOI OK].
- *Why:* upgrades the T7 linear-recurrence family to a real T3 claim in k (Cayley–Hamilton), so it is not bloat.
- *Traps:* two parameters (fix N, scale k); bit costs if not reduced mod 2^64.

**14. Characteristic polynomial.**
- *Costs:* cofactor expansion of det(xI − A) (factorial) → Faddeev–LeVerrier Θ(n⁴) (needs p > n) → Hessenberg
  reduction Θ(n³); Berkowitz Θ(n⁴) is division-free.
- *Sources:* Berkowitz 1984, IPL, doi:10.1016/0020-0190(84)90018-8 [DOI OK]. A source for the Hessenberg method
  was not verified; cite a numerical-linear-algebra or computer-algebra textbook by ISBN.
- *Verifier:* p(r) = det(rI − A) at random r, using the existing Gaussian-elimination code (independent).
- *Traps:* ρ = 1.333 for n⁴ → n³ (caught, margin 0.083); the arithmetic is over GF(p).

**15–16. Union–find; Fenwick tree.**
- *Sources:* Tarjan 1975, J. ACM, doi:10.1145/321879.321884 [DOI OK]; Fenwick 1994, Software: Practice and
  Experience, doi:10.1002/spe.4380240306 [DOI OK].
- *Why:* data structures are underrepresented (one provisional entry).
- *Traps:* α(n) and log factors cannot be resolved, but the n² baselines can; worst-case operation sequences
  must be constructed (quick-find: always relabel the larger set).

**17. Selection.**
- *Costs:* repeated minimum Θ(n·k) (k = n/2) → quickselect Θ(n) expected → median of medians Θ(n) deterministic.
- *Sources:* Hoare 1961, *Algorithm 65: find*, CACM, doi:10.1145/366622.366647 [DOI OK]; Blum et al. 1973, *Time
  bounds for selection*, JCSS, doi:10.1016/S0022-0000(73)80033-9 [DOI OK].
- *Why:* gives the dataset a classical, non-query T4 pair at linear time (randomized vs deterministic Θ(n)).
- *Traps:* quickselect's bound is expected (pivot must be random); median of medians has large constants, so
  check where it crosses over.

**18. Convex hull.**
- *Source:* Graham 1972, IPL, doi:10.1016/0020-0190(72)90045-2 [DOI OK].
- *Verifier:* independent validity check: every input point lies on or left of each hull edge, the hull is

- *Traps:* collinear points and duplicates; log factor (Chan's O(n log h) is not resolvable against n log n).

**19. Smallest enclosing circle.**
- *Source:* Welzl 1991, *Smallest enclosing disks (balls and ellipsoids)*, LNCS, doi:10.1007/BFb0038202 [DOI

- *Why:* introduces `random-order` (randomized incremental construction with backward analysis).
- *Traps:* exact rational arithmetic (Fractions) for integer inputs; Las Vegas expected time, so average over

**20. Bridges / articulation points.**
- *Source:* Tarjan 1972, *Depth-First Search and Linear Graph Algorithms*, SIAM J. Comput.,

**21. Freivalds' verification.**
- *Source:* Motwani & Raghavan 1995, *Randomized Algorithms*, CUP, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511814075 [DOI OK].

- *Traps:* a decision problem, so families need both answers (perturb one entry of C); the V1 check is

**22. Exchange-argument scheduling.**
- *Sources:* Johnson 1954, NRLQ, doi:10.1002/nav.3800010110 [DOI OK]; Smith 1956, NRLQ,

**23. Partition numbers.**
- *Costs:* enumeration costs at least p(n) ~ e^(π√(2n/3))/(4n√3) (Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotics [recalled]), a

- *Source:* Andrews 1984, *The Theory of Partitions*, CUP, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511608650 [DOI OK].
- *Traps:* n is a value (like Fibonacci: all three algorithms are exponential in log n); p(n) has Θ(√n) digits,

**24. Three collinear points.**

**25. Maximum independent set by branching.**

  max degree ≤ 2 directly. T(n) ≤ T(n−1) + T(n−4) gives 1.3803^n (dominant root of x⁴ = x³ + 1; the generator's
  `growth_rate` computes such roots).
- *Sources:* Fomin & Kratsch 2010, *Exact Exponential Algorithms*, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-16533-7 [DOI OK];
  Tarjan & Trojanowski 1977, *Finding a Maximum Independent Set*, SIAM J. Comput., doi:10.1137/0206038 [DOI OK];
  best known O(1.1996^n): Xiao & Nagamochi 2017, doi:10.1016/j.ic.2017.06.001 [DOI OK; the number is recalled].
- *Why:* `branch-and-reduce`, the technique behind most exact exponential algorithms, is absent from the dataset.
- *Traps:* **the bound is an upper bound**. Random graphs make the algorithm look far faster, and a family that
  attains 1.3803^n is not obvious. V2 can honestly claim only "measured growth ≤ claimed" unless a tight family
  is constructed. This is the model case for generator G6.

**26. Ukkonen's banded edit distance.**
- *Source:* Ukkonen 1985, *Algorithms for approximate string matching*, Information and Control,
  doi:10.1016/S0019-9958(85)80046-2 [DOI OK].
- *Traps:* output-sensitive, so instance families must control the distance k (fix k or set k = √n).

**27. Divisor summatory function D(x) = Σ_{k≤x} ⌊x/k⌋.**
- *Costs:* Θ(x) → Dirichlet hyperbola Θ(√x) → Θ̃(x^(1/3)). In bits: 2^n → 2^(n/2) → 2^(n/3).
- *Sources:* Apostol 1976, *Introduction to Analytic Number Theory*, doi:10.1007/978-1-4757-5579-4 [DOI OK]
  (hyperbola method; section not checked); Sladkey 2012, arXiv:1206.3369 [arXiv id OK; the x^(1/3) cost is from
  the title topic and my memory, not checked].
- *Traps:* the T6 primary tag needs confirmation that no polylog(x) algorithm is known (not checked); exact
  integer square roots (`math.isqrt`).

**28. Lehman's factoring method.**
- *Costs:* trial division Θ(N^(1/2)) → Lehman O(N^(1/3)), deterministic and proven [recalled].
- *Source:* Lehman 1974, *Factoring large integers*, Math. Comp., doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1974-0340163-2 [DOI OK].
- *Policy question:* START_HERE section 10 catalogues factoring as T6 and "not targeted operationally". A
  textbook O(N^(1/3)) method on N < 2^64 is not cryptanalysis, but the maintainer should decide.
- *Traps:* the boundary cases of Lehman's ranges must be implemented exactly; Pollard rho must not be added as a
  "proven" algorithm (its running time is heuristic, per the staging entry).

**29–31. Viterbi; CYK; Karp's minimum mean cycle.**
- *Sources:* Viterbi 1967, doi:10.1109/TIT.1967.1054010 [DOI OK]; Younger 1967, doi:10.1016/S0019-9958(67)80007-X
  [DOI OK]; Karp 1978, doi:10.1016/0012-365X(78)90011-0 [DOI OK].
- *Why:* memoisation and characterisation-theorem pairs in domains the dataset lacks (signal decoding, formal
  languages, cycle problems).
- *Traps:* use integer (min-sum) costs for exactness in Viterbi; define CYK's slow baseline as split recursion
  over a CNF grammar (top-down parsing may not terminate on left recursion); Karp's theorem needs exact
  rationals.

**32–33. Levinson; Wiedemann.**
- *Sources:* Levinson 1946, doi:10.1002/sapm1946251261 [DOI OK]; Wiedemann 1986, doi:10.1109/TIT.1986.1057137
  [DOI OK].
- *Traps:* Levinson over GF(p) breaks down when a leading principal minor is singular, so generate instances
  with non-singular minors and say so. The Wiedemann comparison is against **dense** elimination: sparse
  elimination with good pivoting can be much faster, so choose matrices with large fill-in and state the
  baseline honestly. Wiedemann is randomized.

**34. Mean estimation / approximate counting (query model).**
- *Costs:* classical Θ(1/ε²) samples → amplitude estimation Θ(1/ε) queries.
- *Sources:* Brassard, Høyer, Mosca & Tapp 2002, doi:10.1090/conm/305/05215 [DOI OK]; quantum lower bound: Nayak &
  Wu 1999, doi:10.1145/301250.301349 [DOI OK; the exact statement is recalled].
- *Traps:* the parameter is ε, not n (use n = log2(1/ε)); success is probabilistic, so V1 must be statistical;
  the classical Ω(1/ε²) lower bound needs a source (not found tonight); qsim needs controlled Grover iterates and
  an inverse QFT.

**35. Order finding (query model).**
- *Source:* Cleve 2004, *The query complexity of order-finding*, Information and Computation,
  doi:10.1016/j.ic.2004.04.001 [DOI OK; the exact classical bound is recalled]; Shor 1997 (in the dataset).
- *Traps:* the black-box model must match Cleve's; simulation of 2n qubits limits n to about 8–10; continued
  fractions are classical post-processing.

**36–40.**
- **Suffix array.** Manber & Myers 1993, doi:10.1137/0222058; Kasai et al. 2001, doi:10.1007/3-540-48194-X_17
  [both DOI OK]. Trap: Python's C-level string comparison makes the naive sort look far faster than its
  character-comparison cost (a model subtlety).
- **Triangle listing.** Chiba & Nishizeki 1985, doi:10.1137/0214017 [DOI OK]. Trap: big-integer bitset tricks
  give word-level parallelism outside the RAM model.
- **2D linear programming.** Seidel 1991, doi:10.1007/BF02574699 [DOI OK]. Traps: degeneracy and unboundedness.
- **SMAWK.** Aggarwal et al. 1987, doi:10.1007/BF01840359; Hirschberg & Larmore 1987, doi:10.1137/0216043 [both
  DOI OK]. **Input-model trap:** O(n) presumes an implicitly given matrix with O(1) entry evaluation, since an
  explicit matrix already has n² input.
- **General matching.** Edmonds 1965, doi:10.4153/CJM-1965-045-4 [DOI OK]. The blossom algorithm is error-prone;
  the Tutte–Berge formula gives an independent certificate.

### 3.3 Existing-entry extensions (not new pairs)

## 4. Where could genuinely NEW pairs come from?

A "new pair" here means a new, verifiable asymptotic statement, not a rediscovery. START_HERE section 6 applies
throughout: a search finds a faster algorithm only if one exists, and **finding nothing never proves that none
exists**. A null result is logged as NULL with its exact scope (CONTRIBUTING), and it carries no evidence of
impossibility. Impossibility comes only from a proven lower bound in a model that contains the whole search
space. In that case the bound explains the search's failure; the failure is not itself evidence.

### 4.1 Search spaces that can yield verifiable new asymptotic statements

**(a) Bilinear schemes amplified by recursion (matrix multiplication and other bilinear maps).** A scheme for
⟨n,n,n⟩ with r multiplications, verified exactly (Brent equations), gives ω ≤ log_n r. Thresholds
[exp: mm_thresholds]:

| Format | to beat log2 7 ≈ 2.8074 | to beat log4 47 ≈ 2.7773 (GF(2)) | to beat ω < 2.371339 | best rank verifiable from cited titles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2×2 | r ≤ 6 | r ≤ 6 | r ≤ 5 | 7 (rank 7 is optimal: Winograd 1971, doi:10.1016/0024-3795(71)90009-7; Hopcroft & Kerr 1971, doi:10.1137/0120004; both DOI OK, statement recalled) |
| 3×3 | r ≤ 21 | r ≤ 21 | r ≤ 13 | 23: Laderman 1976, title "... using 23 multiplications", doi:10.1090/S0002-9904-1976-13988-2 [DOI OK] |
| 4×4 | r ≤ 48 | r ≤ 46 | r ≤ 26 | 49 = 7² (Strassen on 2×2 blocks); 47 over GF(2) (AlphaTensor [entry]) |
| 5×5 | r ≤ 91 | r ≤ 87 | r ≤ 45 | [recalled] improved 5×5 schemes found by flip-graph search are reported by Kauers & Moosbauer (ISSAC 2023, doi:10.1145/3597066.3597120 [DOI OK]) and Moosbauer & Poole (arXiv:2502.04514 [arXiv id OK]); only the titles were checked, not the ranks |

Consequences:
- **For the parallel flip-graph search:** log_3 22 = 2.8136 > log2 7 [exp: mm_thresholds]. A rank-22 3×3 scheme
  would be a new record rank, but it would **not** give a better exponent than Strassen. Rank ≤ 21 would.
- **Improving ω itself with a small square format is out of reach.** The required ranks (≤ 13 for 3×3, ≤ 26 for
  4×4) are far below the best known. If Bläser's lower bound of (5/2)n² − 3n is right (title of doi:10.1109/SFFCS.1999.814576
  [DOI title OK, no year] states a 5/2 n² bound; the lower-order term is recalled), then no square format below
  8×8 can improve ω at all [exp: mm_thresholds, conditional]. Bläser 2003 (doi:10.1016/S0885-064X(02)00007-9
  [DOI OK]) is recalled to give 19 for 3×3, which would also rule out 3×3.
- **The realistic value of such a search:** verified schemes that beat the exponent of a *named* scheme for one
  format (Strassen, or AlphaTensor's 47 in characteristic 2), recorded as T3 entries with characteristic-specific
  problem statements. The bound to beat for the laser method, ω < 2.371339, is the one stated in the Strassen
  entry (Alman et al., arXiv:2404.16349 [entry]).

**(b) Branching algorithms with computer-generated case analysis (exact exponential and parameterized
algorithms).** A search over local reduction and branching rules, with exhaustive verification of each rule on
all local configurations up to a size bound, yields provable bounds c^n via branching recurrences. Precedent:
Gramm, Guo, Hüffner & Niedermeier 2004, *Automated Generation of Search Tree Algorithms for Hard Graph
Modification Problems*, Algorithmica, doi:10.1007/s00453-004-1090-5 [DOI OK]. Records such a search would have
to beat:
- maximum independent set O(1.1996^n) (Xiao & Nagamochi 2017 [DOI OK, number recalled]);
- vertex cover O(1.2738^k + kn) (Chen, Kanj & Xia 2010, doi:10.1016/j.tcs.2010.06.026 [DOI OK, number recalled]);
- 3-SAT O(1.307^n) (biased-PPSZ; staging entry [entry]).

Realistic outputs are rediscoveries of simple bounds (1.3803^n, 2^(n/3) by Tarjan–Trojanowski [DOI OK]), which
are still useful as verified T8 pairs.

**(c) Composition of small query-complexity separations.** The NAND tree is the composition of the 2-bit NAND:
deterministic 2^h vs randomized 1.686^h leaves (probe, candidate 5). A search over Boolean functions on k ≤ 6–8
bits, with exactly computed deterministic query complexity (dynamic programming over subcubes) and explicit
randomized strategies, could produce new composed families with verified separations. Caveats: that
deterministic complexity multiplies under composition is [recalled], and randomized lower bounds do not compose
in general [recalled]. The best known separations (e.g. pointer functions, Ambainis et al. 2017,
doi:10.1145/3106234 [DOI OK]) are far stronger than what small functions would give, so the products are new
verified *pairs*, not new records.

**(d) Small structured constructions with a recursion theorem in other bilinear settings.** Polynomial and
integer multiplication are essentially settled: O(n log n) bit complexity (Harvey–van der Hoeven 2021, in the
dataset [entry]), so a search can improve at most lower-order factors.

### 4.2 Search spaces that cannot yield new asymptotic pairs, and why

- **Fixed-size programs with no amplification theorem:** sorting networks for fixed k, AlphaDev-style routines,
  addition chains for fixed exponents. Their improvements are constant factors (CONTRIBUTING decision 2;
  notes/constant-factor-alphadev.md). I know of no theorem that turns a better fixed-size sorting network into a
  better asymptotic bound [recalled; not a proven impossibility].
- **Models with proven lower bounds that contain the whole search space.** In these the search's failure is
  explained in advance:
  - monotone (AND/OR) circuits for Boolean matrix product need cubic size: Mehlhorn & Galil 1976,
    doi:10.1007/BF02241983; Paterson 1975, doi:10.1016/0304-3975(75)90009-2 [both DOI OK; statements recalled].
    A search over monotone programs therefore cannot rediscover Strassen; cancellation (subtraction) is necessary.
  - comparison sorting needs Ω(n log n) (sorting entry [entry]);
  - algebraic computation trees for element distinctness need Ω(n log n) (Ben-Or 1983 [entry]);
  - generic-group discrete log needs Ω(√p) (Shoup 1997 [entry]);
  - black-box quantum search needs Ω(√N) (BBBV 1997 [entry]).
- **NP-hard problems to polynomial time:** this would settle P vs NP; a general converter cannot exist
  (START_HERE section 6). Searches here are for better exponents (T8), not for polynomial algorithms.
- **Anything without an exact verifier,** such as approximately correct neural "algorithms" (START_HERE section 6).

## 5. Pair generators

Modelled on `generators/linear_recurrence.py` (input spec → complete entry: implementations, harness,
`entry.json` with automatically derived cost claims, README). There are two honestly different classes:

- **Mechanical transformations** (class M): un-memoising, bloating, re-instantiating a known template. The output
  is **T7**, lives in `synthetic/` and is never counted (START_HERE section 5). It is useful as verified training
  data for "un-bloating" and as regression tests for the validator.
- **Search with an exact verifier** (class S): the generator *finds* something (a scheme, a rule set, an instance
  family) and verifies it exactly. Its outputs can be T2/T3/T8 entries, but almost always as rediscoveries. A
  genuinely new result needs a literature check and the maintainer's review.

### 5.1 Reusable gates (proposed for every generator)

1. **Resolvability gate** (from [exp: v2_resolvability]). Before writing a V2 claim, compute ρ between each pair
   of claimed costs over the planned n range. If |ρ − 1| ≤ 0.25, write only V1 for that separation and say why.
   This would have flagged Strassen (1.069) and subset convolution (1.205) automatically.
2. **Subproblem profiler** (from [exp: subproblem_redundancy]). Trace the slow recursion with `sys.setprofile`
   and count distinct memo keys. Polynomial key growth means "memoisation suffices" (a T2 template; T7 when
   generated). Exponential key growth means another insight is required (Section 1.2).
3. **Deduplication gate.** Canonicalise outputs by (technique, growth rate of each algorithm). Example: the naive
   growth rate depends only on the support of the coefficients, so linear-recurrence-c2-3 duplicates Fibonacci
   (both λ = φ [entry]).
4. **Cost derivation from structure, never from timing.** Claims come from exact call or operation counts or from
   recurrence roots. Fitting timings to obtain a claim would make V2 circular.

### 5.2 Designs

**G1. Recurrence un-memoiser (class M; generalises linear_recurrence.py).**
- *Input:* a recurrence in a small DSL: state tuple and ranges, base cases, a combine operator
  (min/max/+/sum mod p) over child states with weights.
- *Output:* naive recursion, memoised DP, and (if the state is one-dimensional and linear) the companion-matrix
  power.
- *Cost claims:* the exact naive call count C(n) is computed by a counting DP over the same state graph, and the
  V2 claim uses C(n) (reported counts, or time against C(n)); the DP cost = number of states × fan-in.
- *Verifier:* agreement, base cases, and closed forms where known.
- *Traps:* every output repeats fibonacci / edit-distance / matrix-chain; pseudo-polynomial state spaces (a
  weight dimension) must be rejected or flagged; deduplicate by state-graph shape.

**G2. Semiring instantiator (class M).**
- *Input:* one DP skeleton (layered DAG/trellis paths, interval DP/CYK, subset DP) × a semiring (min-plus,
  max-plus, sum-product mod p, Boolean, max-min).
- *Output:* enumeration of all structures (exponential, the natural definition) vs the DP.
- *Cost claims:* derived from the skeleton, independent of the semiring.
- *Verifier:* enumeration for small n, plus cross-semiring identities (Boolean result = [count > 0];
  sum-product with unit weights = number of structures).
- *Class:* T7 by default. The maintainer may promote one instance that matches a named literature problem with
  its own source (e.g. Viterbi = max-plus trellis), and then it is a hand-curated entry using generator
  scaffolding.
- *Traps:* the semiring axioms (distributivity) must be checked, exhaustively on small carriers; floating-point
  semirings are excluded.

**G3. Monoid powering (class M).**
- *Input:* an associative operation (matrices over a semiring, permutation composition, polynomial product
  mod (f, p), composition of functions on [m]).
- *Output:* repeated application Θ(e) vs binary powering Θ(log e) (plus sliding window).
- *Verifier:* associativity, exhaustive for small carriers and randomized otherwise (then probabilistic, said so).
- *Traps:* value-vs-bits (as in Fibonacci and modexp); growth of the operation's cost if values are not reduced.

**G4. Covering counts by inclusion–exclusion (class M, with literature promotion).**
- *Input:* a downward-closed set family given by a predicate (independent sets, matchings, cliques) and the
  question "is U covered by k members?" or "count the coverings".
- *Output:* 3^n subset DP or enumeration vs inclusion–exclusion with a zeta transform Θ(n·2^n).
- *Source for promotion:* Björklund, Husfeldt & Koivisto 2009, *Set Partitioning via Inclusion-Exclusion*, SIAM
  J. Comput., doi:10.1137/070683933 [DOI OK].
- *Traps:* counts overflow, and computing mod p can give a false zero, so use random primes (probabilistic) or
  exact integers; chromatic number is already an entry, so deduplicate.

**G5. Bilinear-scheme amplifier (class S, consumes the flip-graph search).**
- *Input:* a scheme (U, V, W) for ⟨n,m,p⟩ over a ring (e.g. GF(2)), verified by `search.gf2mm.verify`.
- *Output:* a recursive multiplication implementation templated from the scheme; claimed exponent
  3 log r / log(nmp); exact verifier (the Brent equations, plus Freivalds on outputs).
- *V2 plan:* exact scalar-multiplication counts via an instrumented ring element (Section 7, idea I1), since
  timing cannot resolve exponent differences below 0.25 (Strassen: ρ = 1.069).
- *Class:* a T3 entry only if the exponent beats a named scheme; otherwise a methodology note.
- *Traps:* characteristic-specific schemes (GF(2) ⇒ the problem statement must say "over GF(2)"); additions
  change constants and crossover, not the exponent; rank-22 3×3 does not beat log2 7 (Section 4.1).

**G6. Branching-rule synthesiser (class S).**
- *Input:* a problem (maximum independent set, vertex cover, d-hitting set), a language of local configurations
  (degree patterns within radius 2), and candidate reduction and branching rules.
- *Output:* an algorithm plus branching vectors; a bound c^n from the dominant root (generalise `growth_rate`
  in linear_recurrence.py to weighted branching vectors); correctness obligations checked exhaustively on all
  local configurations up to a size bound; V1 against brute force; V2 on reported branching-node counts.
- *Traps:* the bound is an **upper** bound, and V2 on random graphs will look much better than the claim, so the
  claim must be stated as "≤" unless a tight family is generated; measure-and-conquer weights come from numerical
  optimisation and must be re-verified in exact arithmetic; beating the recorded bounds (Section 4.1 b) with small
  rule sets is very unlikely, so treat outputs as rediscoveries.

**G7. Query-function composer (class S).**
- *Input:* a Boolean function f on k ≤ 6–8 bits.
- *Output:* exact deterministic query complexity (subcube DP), an explicit randomized strategy with its exact
  expected cost on the worst input (small LP or enumeration), and composed families f^∘d with reported counts.
- *Traps:* lower bounds for randomized composition need proofs per family [recalled: no general composition
  theorem]; hard input distributions must be generated per family (candidate 5 shows how).

**G8. Reduction transfer (class M).**
- *Input:* an existing pair for problem B and a verified linear-time reduction A ≤ B (LIS ≤ LCS, already used as
  an oracle in the LIS entry; 2-SAT ≤ SCC; element distinctness ≤ sorting).
- *Output:* slow = native brute force for A; fast = reduction + B's fast algorithm.
- *Traps:* instance blow-up; reductions destroy B's worst-case families, so worst cases must be generated in A's
  space; T7 unless the composite is itself a literature algorithm.

**G9. Worst-case instance search (class S, a V2 support tool).** Hill-climb over small instances to maximise an
algorithm's exact operation count, then generalise the resulting family by hand (with proof). RL-005 (Dijkstra's
early exit) and RL-018 (Hungarian on random matrices) show that instance choice decides V2. Trap: small-n
artefacts; the output is evidence, not proof.

## 7. Open ideas for later

**I1. Exact operation counts as a V2 measure for classical entries (proposal for a DECISION).**
- *Motivation:* the resolvability script shows that the ±0.25 slope test cannot separate Strassen (1.069),
  Toom-3 (1.082) or subset convolution at small n (1.205), and RL-006 recorded the same for Strassen by timing.
- *Idea:* count scalar multiplications with an instrumented ring element whose `__mul__` increments a counter.
  This is independent of the algorithm's code: the algorithm cannot under-report. Then check the count against
  the claimed closed form (e.g. exactly 7^k for Strassen with cutoff 1 at n = 2^k), not just a slope.
- *Trap:* operation counts ignore memory and interpreter effects, so they complement timing rather than replace
  it; the claimed closed form must come from analysis.

**I2. Hypothesis: "memoisation sufficiency" can be classified automatically.**
- *Evidence:* the profiler separated 4 memoisation-sufficient from 2 insufficient slow recursions (Section 1.2).
- *Test:* run it on every new recursive baseline, and check that "insufficient" always coincides with an extra
  structural insight in the fast algorithm.

**I3. Hypothesis: T8 improvements come from a small set of properties.**
- *Evidence:* four of the five T8 pairs in pairs/ use subset-dp, inclusion–exclusion or meet-in-the-middle; the
  fifth (3-SAT, added during the session) uses randomized local search [exp: dataset_stats].
- *Prediction:* the next T8 pairs will use these four mechanisms or branch-and-reduce (candidate 25), and nothing
  else.
- *Caveat:* the 3-SAT entry is not independent support, because I knew it was being implemented when I wrote
  this hypothesis.
- *Falsifier:* a T8 pair whose improvement uses none of these five.

**I4. Boundary-pair collection as negative examples.** Problems that look alike but sit on opposite sides of a
complexity cliff:
- 2-SAT / 3-SAT (note exists);
- determinant / permanent (in the entries);
- longest common substring / subsequence (candidates 7 vs lcs);
- Euler / Hamilton cycles;
- two- / three-machine flow shop;
- planar / general perfect-matching counting.
For a learned proposer these are the cases where surface similarity misleads.

**I5. Cross-model technique graph.** The Hadamard transform appears in Bernstein–Vazirani, Deutsch–Jozsa, Simon,
Forrelation (quantum) and in the FWHT (classical, candidate 1). Linking entries by shared transforms could help
the dequantisation question (T5): where the classical transform suffices given comparable input access.

**I6. Automated worst-case families for V2** (G9), motivated by RL-005 and RL-018.

**Directions dropped, with reasons:**
- Embedding-based similarity mining over entries: 54 entries are too few for it to beat a hand mapping, and its
  output would be unverifiable.
- Deriving cost claims by fitting timings: circular (V2 must test an independently derived claim).
- Un-memoising arbitrary DP source code automatically: needs program analysis. G1 works from a recurrence
  specification instead, which is simpler and checkable.
- Mass literature mining through the arXiv API: rate limits and the project rule (a handful of queries); one
  batched call was used.
